## Day 1 · Welcome

**Key idea:** anxiety can run like a habit, and habits can change. · **Task:** catch worry switching on.

**Hook**

It's 11 pm. You're done for the day, but your mind isn't. It replays a conversation, checks tomorrow for problems, then starts again from the top. You didn't decide to do this. It just happens.

**Idea 1 — It's not a flaw in you**

That late-night spin can feel like proof that something is wrong with you. It's more ordinary than that. Your brain is doing something it has practiced many times, the same way your hand reaches for your phone without asking you first.

**Idea 2 — Anxiety can run on autopilot**

A lot of anxiety works like a habit. Something sets it off, you respond in a familiar way, and you get a brief payoff. Each time the cycle repeats, it gets a little more automatic.

**Idea 3 — Habits can be relearned**

The good news about habits is that they were learned, so they can be relearned. You don't need to force the anxiety away. Over the next 30 days you'll learn to see the cycle clearly, and that alone starts to loosen it.

**Example — Just to be safe**

Priya checks her work inbox every evening "just to be safe." Most nights she finds nothing urgent, yet she feels tense for an hour afterward. Checking has become a habit that promises calm and rarely delivers it.

**Try it now (30 s)**

Think of one moment this week when worry switched on by itself. Where were you? What time was it? Just picture it. There's nothing to fix yet.

**Today's task**

Sometime today, notice one moment when worry starts up on its own. You don't need to do anything about it. Just say to yourself, "There it is."

**Footer (Day 1 only)**

Loosening Worry is a self-help course, not a treatment or a diagnosis. If anxiety is making daily life very hard, or you're in crisis, open Help for support lines.

## Day 2 · Your brain loves loops

**Key idea:** every habit has a trigger, a behavior and a result. · **Task:** log your first loop.

**Hook**

You unlock your phone to check the time. Ten minutes later you're deep in a feed, and you still don't know what time it is.

**Idea 1 — Three parts, every time**

Habits run as small loops with three parts. A **trigger** sets it off: a place, a feeling, a thought. A **behavior** follows: the thing you do. Then comes a **result**: what you get, or what you feel, right afterward.

**Idea 2 — The result keeps it going**

Your brain watches the result closely. If the behavior brought even a moment of relief, distraction or comfort, your brain files it as "worth repeating." Next time the trigger shows up, the behavior is a little quicker to follow.

**Idea 3 — Seeing the parts is step one**

Most loops run too fast to notice. Breaking one into its three parts slows it down, so you can see what's actually happening instead of just being carried along.

**Example — "Got a minute?"**

Sam's manager sends a short message: "Got a minute?" (trigger). Sam spends the next half hour imagining what went wrong (behavior). Sam feels drained and on edge, and the meeting turns out to be about holiday dates (result).

**Try it now (45 s)**

Pick the phone example or one of your own. Say each part to yourself: "The trigger was… I did… Afterwards I felt…"

**Today's task**

When you catch one today, tap Log and choose a trigger, a behavior and a result. It doesn't need to be a worry loop yet. Any habit counts.

## Day 3 · Worry as a behavior

**Key idea:** worrying is something we do, not just something we feel. · **Task:** log one worry loop.

**Hook**

You've read the email five times. Nothing in it has changed, but you're still searching it for a hidden meaning.

**Idea 1 — Feeling vs. doing**

Anxiety often arrives as a feeling: a tight chest, a fluttery stomach. Worry is what we tend to *do* next. We replay, rehearse, predict and check. It feels like it's happening to us, but it's an action the mind takes.

**Idea 2 — Worry fits the loop**

That means worry can sit in the behavior slot of a loop. An uneasy feeling is the trigger. Worrying is the behavior. The result is often more unease, which becomes the trigger for the next round.

**Idea 3 — Why this helps**

You can't simply switch a feeling off. But you can notice a behavior, and a behavior you notice is one you have some choice about. Calling worry a behavior gives you somewhere to start.

**Example — No reply yet**

Leo feels a twinge of unease when his friend doesn't reply to a text (trigger). He scrolls back through the chat, guessing what he said wrong (behavior). He ends up more uneasy than before, and checks again (result).

**Try it now (45 s)**

Think of a recent worry. Instead of asking "What was I worried about?", ask "What was I *doing*?" Replaying? Planning for the worst? Checking? Pick the word that fits.

**Today's task**

Log one loop where worrying is the behavior. If you're not sure, choose "Overthought it" and keep going. A rough log is better than none.

*Button: Log a loop*

## Day 4 · Finding your triggers

**Key idea:** triggers can be places, times, people or thoughts. · **Task:** note 3 triggers you noticed today.

**Hook**

Sunday evening rolls around and your mood drops, even though nothing bad has happened yet. The week ahead hasn't started, but your body already knows it's coming.

**Idea 1 — Triggers are everywhere**

A trigger is anything that sets a loop in motion. Some are outside you: a place, a time of day, a notification, a person's name on the screen. Others are inside: a thought, a memory, a sensation like a racing heart.

**Idea 2 — They're often small**

Triggers are rarely dramatic. A single word in a message, the sound of keys in the door, or simply feeling tired can be enough. Because they're small, they slip past us, and the loop seems to start from nowhere.

**Idea 3 — Spotting them gives you a head start**

When you know your common triggers, they stop being ambushes. You can see one coming and think, "Ah, this is where it usually starts." That moment of recognition is where change begins.

**Example — Three start points**

Maya noticed her worry loops cluster at three points: opening her banking app, the walk into the office, and lying down at night. Seeing the pattern helped her realize the worry wasn't random. It had regular start points.

**Try it now (45 s)**

Scan your day so far. Pick one trigger from each group if you can: a **place**, a **time**, a **person**, a **thought**. Don't judge them. Just name them.

**Today's task**

Notice three triggers today. Log each one when you catch it, and pick your best guess for the behavior and result. The trigger is today's focus.

## Day 5 · Why worry feels useful

**Key idea:** worry gives a feeling of control, not actual control. · **Task:** log a loop and name what you hoped worry would do.

**Hook**

The night before a flight, you mentally run through everything that could go wrong. Somehow it feels irresponsible *not* to.

**Idea 1 — Worry makes a promise**

Worry sticks around because it seems to offer something. It promises that if you think hard enough, you'll be ready, you'll stay safe, or you won't be caught off guard. That promise is the payoff your brain is chasing.

**Idea 2 — Feeling in control isn't being in control**

While you're worrying, you feel busy and alert, as if you're handling the problem. But most of the time nothing actually gets solved. The plane flies the same whether you worry or not. You just arrive more tired.

**Idea 3 — Name the promise**

You don't have to argue with worry. Start by asking what it's promising you this time: Safety? Being prepared? Avoiding blame? Once you can name the promise, you can start checking whether it keeps it.

**Example — Twenty useful minutes**

Aisha worries about her presentation every evening for a week. She tells herself it's preparation. When she looks back, the useful part took 20 minutes: writing her notes. The other six evenings were replays that left her no more ready.

**Try it now (45 s)**

Think of a recent worry. Finish this sentence: "If I keep worrying about this, then…" Whatever comes next is the promise.

**Today's task**

Log a worry loop, then add a note naming what you hoped the worry would do for you. Keep it to a few words, like "keep me safe" or "be ready."

## Day 6 · Avoidance loops

**Key idea:** avoiding brings relief now and more anxiety later. · **Task:** log one thing you put off.

**Hook**

The letter from the bank has been on the counter for a week. You haven't opened it. Every time you walk past, your stomach tightens a little.

**Idea 1 — Avoiding is a behavior too**

Not every loop is busy thinking. Sometimes the behavior is stepping away: not opening the message, leaving the call for later, changing the subject. It can look like doing nothing, but it fills the behavior slot just the same.

**Idea 2 — The relief is real, and short**

Putting something off brings a quick wave of relief. Your brain files that relief as the result, which is why avoiding feels so right in the moment. But the thing you avoided is still there, and so is the worry about it, often a little bigger.

**Idea 3 — Look at the whole result**

The trick is to look past the first few seconds. What did avoiding give you an hour later? A day later? When you see the full result, not just the first moment of relief, the loop starts to lose its pull.

**Example — The unanswered invite**

Tom's friend invites him to a party where he'll know almost no one (trigger). He leaves the message unanswered for three days (behavior). Each day brings a small relief, then a bigger knot of guilt, and by the weekend the reply feels harder to write than ever (result).

**Try it now (45 s)**

Think of one thing you've been putting off. Ask yourself: "What did avoiding it give me right away? And what has it given me since?" Notice both answers.

**Today's task**

Log one loop where you avoided something or put it off. Choose "Avoided it" or "Put it off." For the result, pick what it gave you later, not just in the first minute.

## Day 7 · Your loop map

**Key idea:** patterns show up when you look back. · **Task:** review your loops in Insights.

**Hook**

One loop on its own looks random. A week of them starts to look like a map.

**Idea 1 — Each log is a dot**

Every loop you've logged is a dot on a map. One dot doesn't say much. A handful start to show where your loops tend to begin, and when.

**Idea 2 — Look for repeats**

Most people find their loops cluster: the same two or three triggers, the same time of day, the same habit stepping in. These repeats are your most practiced loops, which makes them the most useful to know.

**Idea 3 — Curious, not critical**

A map is just information. A busy week of loops doesn't mean you're doing badly. It means you're noticing more, which is exactly the skill this course builds.

**Example — Where the worry lives**

After a week of logging, Mei opened Insights and saw that most of her loops started between 9 and 11 pm, and "Kept checking" came up again and again. She hadn't realized her evenings were where most of the worry lived.

**Try it now (30 s)**

Before you look, make a guess. Which trigger do you think shows up most? What time of day? Hold your guess in mind.

**Today's task**

Open Insights and look at your week. Check your guess against what's there: your top triggers, when loops tend to start, and what your most common habit gave you. If you've logged only a few loops, that's fine. Look at what's there.

## Day 8 · Habits beyond worry

**Key idea:** snacking, scrolling and over-planning can be anxiety loops too. · **Task:** log a non-worry loop.

**Hook**

It's 4 pm, a hard email has just landed, and you find yourself standing at the fridge. You're not really hungry.

**Idea 1 — Anxiety wears disguises**

Worry isn't the only habit that grows from unease. Scrolling, snacking, tidying, over-planning, a quick search online: any of these can step in when something feels uncomfortable.

**Idea 2 — Same loop, different behavior**

The shape is the same. A trigger brings unease, the behavior offers a quick change of feeling, and the result is what you get. The behavior may look harmless, even pleasant, but it's running the same loop.

**Idea 3 — Not bad, just a loop**

This isn't about cutting out snacks or your phone. It's about seeing when a habit is doing a job for your anxiety. Once you see the job, you can check how well it does it.

**Example — A cookie for every email**

Whenever a hard email landed, Priya went to the kitchen for a cookie. She wasn't hungry; it just took the edge off. Ten minutes later the email was still waiting, and she felt a little sluggish on top of the unease.

**Try it now (45 s)**

Think about the last time you reached for your phone or a snack without deciding to. What happened just before? Was there a small moment of unease?

**Today's task**

Log one loop today where the behavior isn't worrying: scrolling, snacking, searching, tidying or anything else. If it's not on the list, add your own.

## Day 9 · Catch it sooner

**Key idea:** noticing earlier gives you more choice. · **Task:** log a loop as it starts, not after.

**Hook**

Most of the time you notice a loop when it's already over: half an hour of worry gone, and the thought "Why did I do that again?"

**Idea 1 — Late noticing still counts**

Spotting a loop afterward isn't a failure. It's how everyone starts. Each loop you notice after the fact trains your brain to notice the next one a little sooner.

**Idea 2 — Earlier gives you room**

When you catch a loop at the trigger, or in its first minute, there's a small gap before the habit takes over. You don't need to do anything with that gap yet. Just knowing it's there changes things.

**Idea 3 — Learn your early signs**

Loops often announce themselves: a tight jaw, a familiar "What if…", a hand already reaching for the phone. Learning your early signs is like learning the sound of your own front door.

**Example — The first "What if…"**

Zara used to notice her money worry only after an hour of checking her bank app. This week she caught it at the first "What if…" while making coffee, and logged it right there, before the checking started.

**Try it now (30 s)**

What's usually the very first sign that one of your loops is starting? A thought, a feeling in your body, an action? Name one.

**Today's task**

Try to log a loop as it starts, while the trigger is still fresh. If you only catch it afterward, log it anyway, and notice how far in you were when you noticed.

## Day 11 · What did you get?

**Key idea:** your brain repeats what it thinks is rewarding. · **Task:** check the result honestly every time you log today.

**Hook**

You finally put the phone down after twenty minutes of scrolling. Be honest: do you feel better than when you picked it up?

**Idea 1 — Your brain keeps score**

Each time a behavior runs, your brain records what it got. If it scores the result as worth having, it nudges you to repeat the behavior next time. That score is what keeps a habit going.

**Idea 2 — The score can be out of date**

Many scores were set long ago, sometimes in a single moment of relief. Worry may once have felt useful, so your brain still rates it highly, even if today it mostly leaves you tired.

**Idea 3 — Update it by looking**

You can't argue a habit into changing its score. But you can let your brain see what the behavior gives you now, clearly and in the moment. That fresh information is what updates it.

**Example — Twenty minutes of news**

Mia scrolls the news for twenty minutes whenever she feels uneasy, sure it will calm her down. Today she stopped afterwards and asked herself how she felt. The answer: more on edge than before. She'll probably check again tomorrow, but now part of her knows it doesn't help, and each time she notices, the pull gets a little weaker.

**Try it now (45 s)**

Recall a habit you did recently. Feel what it left you with, not what you think it should have left you with. Relief? Tension? Not much at all?

**Today's task**

Log every loop you notice today. At "What did it actually give you?", take one breath and pick what you really feel, even if that's "Not sure."

## Day 12 · Worry is a habit, not a fact

**Key idea:** a worried thought is not a prediction. · **Task:** note one worry that didn't come true.

**Hook**

Your partner is late home, and within two minutes your mind has written a whole story: the accident, the hospital, the phone call. Then the door opens.

**Idea 1 — Worry sounds certain**

Worried thoughts often arrive sounding sure: "This will go badly." "They're upset with me." The certainty is part of the habit. It feels like information, but it's a pattern your mind has practiced.

**Idea 2 — Check the track record**

Think back over this month's worries. How many came true the way the worry said they would? Most people find very few did, and the ones that did were rarely helped by the worrying.

**Idea 3 — A thought, not a fact**

You don't have to prove a worried thought wrong. You can simply see it as a thought: "There's the worry habit again." That small step back makes it easier to hold lightly.

**Example — Two small edits**

Kofi spent the whole weekend convinced his boss would tear his report apart. On Monday the feedback was two small edits and "Thanks, good work." He made a note of it, and the next time the same worry came up, he recognized it.

**Try it now (45 s)**

Think of one thing you worried about last week. What did the worry predict? What actually happened?

**Today's task**

Find one worry that didn't come true, or didn't turn out as badly as it said. Log it as a loop and add a few words in the note, like "didn't happen."

## Day 13 · Feel it in your body

**Key idea:** results show up as body sensations. · **Task:** name where you feel a loop's result.

**Hook**

After an hour of replaying a conversation, you notice your shoulders are up near your ears and your jaw aches.

**Idea 1 — The body keeps the receipts**

A habit's result isn't only a thought like "that didn't help." It's felt: tight shoulders, a heavy stomach, a buzzing chest, tired eyes. These sensations are often more honest than our opinions.

**Idea 2 — Feeling it beats knowing it**

You can know that worry doesn't help and still worry. Feeling the tightness it leaves behind gives your brain the raw information it learns from, and that's what shifts the habit.

**Idea 3 — A quick scan is enough**

You don't need a long exercise. After a loop, take a few seconds to scan from your head down to your stomach and ask: where do I feel this?

**Example — A tight chest**

After checking her phone for messages over and over (behavior), Elena noticed a tight chest and shallow breathing (result). Naming the tightness made the habit's cost feel real in a way thinking about it never had.

**Try it now (45 s)**

Take a breath. Scan your forehead, jaw, shoulders, chest and stomach. Where is there tension right now? Just notice it. There's nothing to fix.

**Today's task**

After the next loop you log, scan your body and name where you feel its result. Add it in the note, like "tight chest" or "heavy stomach."

## Day 14 · Willpower isn't the answer

**Key idea:** forcing yourself to stop rarely lasts. · **Task:** notice one urge without fighting it.

**Hook**

"I'm not going to check again." Five minutes later you're checking again, and now you're annoyed with yourself too.

**Idea 1 — Fighting feeds the loop**

Pushing a habit away takes effort, and effort runs out, especially when you're tired or stressed, which is exactly when loops show up. And when willpower gives way, blaming yourself often becomes a new loop.

**Idea 2 — Get interested instead**

Instead of fighting the urge, you can get interested in it. What does it feel like? Where is it in your body? Does it change as you watch it? Curiosity takes far less effort than resisting.

**Idea 3 — You don't have to stop yet**

This stage isn't about stopping anything. If you end up doing the habit, that's fine. Do it with your eyes open, and notice what it gives you.

**Example — Looking up symptoms**

Ravi's doctor had checked the twinge and told him it was nothing to worry about. Still, whenever he felt it, he'd search his symptoms online. Even after reading it was probably nothing, he'd worry his last search had missed something, so he searched again, and again. For weeks he tried to force himself to stop, and kept giving in. This week he tried something different: when the urge came, he got curious about it instead of fighting it. He noticed how it felt: restless, a pull in his hands. Sometimes he searched anyway. But a few times the urge faded before he'd even picked up his phone.

**Try it now (45 s)**

Think of a habit that tugs at you. Picture the urge arriving. Instead of saying no to it, ask: "What does this urge feel like right now?"

**Today's task**

When an urge comes, watch it for a few breaths. Whatever you do next, log the loop.

## Day 15 · Seeing clearly

**Key idea:** repeated honest looks lower a habit's appeal. · **Task:** check the result of your most common loop.

**Hook**

The first time you notice that checking the news makes you feel worse, it's a small surprise. By the tenth time, the habit starts to feel less tempting.

**Idea 1 — Each look counts**

One honest look at a result won't undo a habit built over years. But each look adds to your brain's tally. Over time the habit's appeal fades on its own, without a fight.

**Idea 2 — Start with your most common loop**

The loops you repeat most carry the highest score in your brain. They also give you the most chances to look, so they're where change tends to show first.

**Idea 3 — Patience, not pressure**

Habits don't drop away in a day. Some days the pull will feel just as strong as ever. That's normal, and the looking is still working underneath.

**Example — The budget spreadsheet**

Hana's most common loop was money worry (trigger) followed by reopening her budget spreadsheet (behavior). She started paying close attention to what it gave her each time. After two weeks she reached for the spreadsheet less, and when she did, she stopped sooner.

**Try it now (30 s)**

What's your most common loop? If you're not sure, Insights shows your top triggers and your most common habit.

**Today's task**

The next time your most common loop shows up, log it and check its result carefully. Take a breath before you choose, and feel what it really gave you.

## Day 16 · The "just one more" trap

**Key idea:** checking again rarely brings the calm you want. · **Task:** catch one re-checking loop.

**Hook**

You've locked the door. You go back to check. It's locked. On the way to the car, you wonder whether you checked properly.

**Idea 1 — Checking promises certainty**

Re-checking the door, your messages, the weather or your bank balance promises one thing: certainty. "Once I know for sure, I'll relax."

**Idea 2 — Certainty doesn't stick**

The relief from a check is real, but it fades fast, often within minutes. Then the doubt comes back, and the urge to check comes with it. Each check can make the next one feel more necessary.

**Idea 3 — Watch the fade**

You don't need to stop checking today. Instead, watch how long the relief lasts. Seeing it fade, again and again, helps your brain update what checking is worth.

**Example — Refresh, refresh**

Waiting to hear back about a job (trigger), Ben refreshed his email every few minutes (behavior). Each refresh brought a flicker of hope, then a drop. When he paid attention, the calm from each check lasted less than a minute (result).

**Try it now (45 s)**

Think of something you tend to check more than once. The last time you checked it, how long did the calm last?

**Today's task**

When you catch one, log it with "Kept checking," and at the result, notice how long the relief actually lasted.

## Day 17 · Self-judgment loops

**Key idea:** being hard on yourself is a loop too. · **Task:** log a self-criticism loop.

**Hook**

You catch yourself worrying again, and straight away another voice joins in: "Why can't you just stop? What's wrong with you?"

**Idea 1 — The second loop**

Many people stack a second loop on top of the first. The trigger is noticing the habit. The behavior is criticizing yourself. The result is usually more tension, which feeds the next round of worry.

**Idea 2 — Criticism makes a promise too**

Like worry, self-judgment promises something: "If I'm hard on myself, I'll do better." Check whether it keeps that promise. For most people, it mostly leaves them feeling smaller and more stuck.

**Idea 3 — Treat it like any other loop**

You don't need to argue with the critical voice. Treat it like any other habit: name it, notice what set it off, and see what it really gives you.

**Example — "A terrible parent"**

Lucia snapped at her son, then spent the evening telling herself she was a terrible parent (behavior). By bedtime she felt heavy and exhausted, and no closer to the calm talk she wanted to have with him (result).

**Try it now (45 s)**

Think of the last time you criticized yourself. What were the words? What tone of voice? How did your body feel afterward?

**Today's task**

Log one self-criticism loop today. If it isn't in your list of behaviors, add your own, like "Criticized myself."

## Day 19 · When it really helps

**Key idea:** some behaviors do solve problems, and that's fine. · **Task:** sort one loop: helpful or not?.

**Hook**

You worried about the tax deadline, so you sat down and filed. The worry went away. Was that a loop?

**Idea 1 — Not every response is a trap**

Sometimes unease leads you to act, and the action solves the problem. Making a plan, sending the email, booking the appointment: these can be genuinely helpful.

**Idea 2 — The difference is in the result**

A helpful response usually ends something. The problem gets handled, and the worry eases and stays eased. A loop tends to repeat: the same thoughts, the same checks, nothing new happening.

**Idea 3 — Honest both ways**

This isn't about calling everything you do a bad habit. It's about seeing clearly. When something helps, notice that too, and pick "It actually helped."

**Example — Two things to say**

Grace was dreading a hard conversation with her sister. She wrote down the two things she wanted to say and called that evening. The worry eased and didn't come back. The same week she noticed another pattern: rehearsing arguments in the shower, which never led anywhere.

**Try it now (45 s)**

Think of something you did about a worry recently. Did it end something, or did the worry come back soon after?

**Today's task**

Log one loop and sort it honestly. If it really helped, choose "It actually helped." If it didn't, notice what it gave you instead.

## Day 20 · Stage 2 check-in

**Key idea:** disenchantment grows with repetition. · **Task:** log the habit that's lost the most shine.

**Hook**

Some habits still pull at you. But a few may feel a little less shiny than they did ten days ago.

**Idea 1 — The shine wears off**

When you keep seeing what a habit really gives you, its appeal slowly fades, a bit like a song you've heard too many times. You don't decide to stop liking it. It just loses its pull.

**Idea 2 — Not all at once**

Some habits loosen quickly. Others take longer, especially the ones you've practiced for years. Both are normal, and the looking is still working.

**Idea 3 — What comes next**

In stage 3 you'll use four tools that give your brain something better to do when a loop starts: curiosity, breathing, noting and kindness. They work best now, after you've seen what the old habits really give you.

**Example — The letdown coming**

Nikhil noticed that "Searched online" no longer felt like relief. He still did it sometimes, but now he could feel the letdown coming before he'd even opened the browser.

**Try it now (45 s)**

## Day 21 · Curiosity beats worry

**Key idea:** curiosity feels better than anxiety, so the brain prefers it. · **Task:** use Get curious once.

**Hook**

Think of the last time you were really curious about something: a mystery, a new place, a puzzle. Notice how different that feels from worry.

**Idea 1 — Your brain wants a better offer**

Your brain repeats what feels rewarding. If worry is the best response it knows to unease, it will keep choosing worry. Changing the habit is easier when there's something on offer that genuinely feels better.

**Idea 2 — Curiosity is that offer**

Curiosity is open and interested. Worry is narrow and tight. When you turn curiosity toward an anxious feeling ("Hmm, what is this?"), you're no longer stuck inside it. You're exploring it.

**Idea 3 — You already have it**

You don't need to learn curiosity. You already have it. The skill is pointing it at the feeling in your body instead of at the worried story.

**Example — "Hmm, what is this?"**

In a stressful meeting, Rosa felt the familiar knot in her stomach. Instead of running through everything that could go wrong, she asked herself, "Hmm, what is this? Where exactly is it?" The knot didn't vanish, but she felt less caught up in it.

**Try it now (45 s)**

Notice any feeling in your body right now, even a small one. Say "Hmm…" and get interested in it. Is it warm or cool? Still or moving?

## Day 23 · Name it to step back

**Key idea:** labeling a thought creates distance from it. · **Task:** use Note it during a loop.

**Hook**

A thought on its own can sweep you away. A thought with a label, like "planning" or "worrying," is something you can see.

## Day 24 · Breath as an anchor

**Key idea:** slow breathing gives the body a calm signal. · **Task:** use Breathe before a stressful moment.

**Hook**

Before a hard phone call, you notice your breath is fast and high in your chest, as if you're already running.

**Idea 1 — Breath talks to the body**

Anxiety speeds the breath up. Slowing it down, especially the out-breath, sends the opposite signal: there's no emergency right now.

**Idea 2 — An anchor, not a fix**

Slow breathing won't make worry disappear. Think of it as an anchor: something steady to come back to when your thoughts start pulling you away.

**Idea 3 — Before helps too**

Breathing helps in the middle of a loop, and it also helps just before a moment you know will be hard. A minute or two beforehand can take the edge off.

**Example — Ten minutes to ten**

Chris had a presentation at 10. At 9:50 they sat in a quiet corner and used Breathe for two minutes: in for four, out for six. Their heart was still beating fast when they began, but they felt steady enough to start.

## Day 26 · Riding the urge

**Key idea:** urges rise and fall like waves if you don't act on them. · **Task:** watch one urge pass.

**Hook**

The urge to check your phone feels like it will keep growing until you give in. Does it, though?

**Idea 1 — Urges are waves**

An urge builds, peaks and then falls, often within a few minutes. It feels as if it will keep growing forever, but it doesn't. The catch is that we usually act at the peak, so we never get to see it fall.

**Idea 2 — Ride it, don't fight it**

You don't have to push the urge away. Watch it the way a surfer watches a wave: notice it build, feel its strongest point, and see it start to ease.

**Idea 3 — Every wave teaches something**

Each time you watch an urge pass without acting on it, your brain learns that urges aren't orders. They come and go on their own.

**Example — "Are we OK?"**

Jack felt a strong pull to text his partner "Are we OK?" for the third time that day. He set a five-minute timer and watched the urge instead. It peaked around the second minute as a tight, restless feeling in his chest. By the fifth minute it was much quieter.

**Try it now (60 s)**

Think of an urge you feel often. Imagine it arriving, and picture it as a wave: building, peaking, then slowly settling. Breathe as you watch it.

**Today's task**

The next time an urge shows up, ride it for a few minutes before you act. Use Breathe if it helps. Notice whether it rises and falls.
